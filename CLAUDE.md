# CLAUDE.md

## Project

This repository is for COMP4020 Assignment 2.

The course being designed is:

**Picture Books That Misbehave**
**Subtitle:** Breaking the Rules of Visual Storytelling

This is a second-year undergraduate studio course about deliberately breaking the normal rules of picture books to create meaningful reading experiences.

Do not turn it into:

* a general history of picture books;
* a children's literature survey;
* an illustration technique course;
* a course mainly about polished drawing;
* a course built around random visual gimmicks.

The central question is:

**What happens when a picture book refuses to behave like a normal book?**

The course should treat picture books as designed systems made from words, images, sequence, page turns, framing, physical pages, reader actions, and narrative expectations.

---

## Core design principle

The course follows this progression:

**Notice -> Break -> Construct -> Test -> Integrate**

Students should first understand a convention, then deliberately break it, then use that break to construct an experience, test it with readers, and finally decide which forms of misbehaviour are worth keeping.

A rule should never be broken only for novelty.

Every act of misbehaviour should create a clear narrative, visual, spatial, or reader effect.

---

## Target students

The course should be understandable and useful to students from areas such as:

* illustration;
* visual communication and design;
* animation;
* interaction design / HCI;
* creative writing;
* media arts;
* comics and sequential art;
* storytelling;
* education and early childhood education.

There is no formal prerequisite.

Students do not need polished drawing skills.

They may work with collage, photography, typography, stick figures, digital shapes, paper cut-outs, found images, or other simple visual methods.

Assessment and course language should reward concept, intention, reader effect, testing, judgement, and revision rather than drawing polish.

Education perspectives may appear where useful, especially around shared reading and different reader attention, but the course must not become a course about teaching with picture books.

---

## Learning outcomes

The course uses these five learning outcomes.

### LO1 - Read the rules

Identify the visual, textual, spatial, and narrative conventions that shape how a picture book is normally read.

### LO2 - Break with purpose

Break a picture-book convention deliberately to create a specific narrative or reading effect.

### LO3 - Design across words, pictures and pages

Use relationships between words, images, sequence, page turns, and the physical page to construct visual narratives.

### LO4 - Design for the reader

Predict and test how readers notice, interpret, navigate, and respond to an unconventional picture book.

### LO5 - Make a coherent misbehaving book

Create and explain an original picture-book work in which rule-breaking supports the story rather than functioning as decoration or novelty.

Do not replace these learning outcomes without an explicit instruction from the user.

## Twelve-week course structure

The course must contain exactly twelve dated teaching weeks.

The weeks form one coherent semester rather than twelve unrelated topics.

The planned progression is:

1. **How Should a Picture Book Behave?**
2. **The Picture Isn't What You Think**
3. **Words and Pictures Disagree**
4. **The Story Is Lying**
5. **The Story Behind the Story**
6. **The Page Is a Place**
7. **Don't Turn the Page**
8. **Read It the Wrong Way**
9. **The Reader Did It**
10. **The Character Refuses**
11. **The Book Knows It's a Book**
12. **Make It Misbehave**

Do not rename, reorder, remove, merge, or replace these weeks unless explicitly asked.

The broad semester structure is:

* Weeks 1-3: **Look Again**
* Weeks 4-5: **Don't Trust the Story**
* Weeks 6-8: **That's Not How Books Work**
* Weeks 9-12: **Who's in Charge Here?**

Narrative deception is only one part of the course. Do not make unreliable narration or plot twists the organising idea for the whole semester.

The course must also include visual and spatial misbehaviour such as framing, cropping, perspective, page architecture, sequence, reader action, and material or metafictional features.

## Weekly structure

Each week should have a clear reason for existing.

When writing or revising weekly content, make sure the week answers:

1. What normal rule or expectation is being examined?
2. What problem or possibility appears when that rule is questioned?
3. What does the student make, notice, test, or try?
4. What should the student understand by the end?
5. Which learning outcome or outcomes does it support?
6. How does it prepare students for the following week?

Do not expose all six questions as repetitive headings on every public page unless that structure genuinely improves the design.

The public website should feel varied and authored, not like twelve copies of the same template with different nouns.

## Weekly Misbehaviour

Every teaching week must contain one distinct activity called:

**This Week's Misbehaviour**

These are low-risk creative experiments.

They should normally:

* take roughly 15-30 minutes, excluding discussion;
* be possible with rough materials;
* test one idea clearly;
* encourage experimentation rather than polish;
* allow an unsuccessful experiment to still be useful when the intention is clear.

The twelve activities should not all have the same format.

They may include making, noticing, comparing, rearranging, testing, or observing.

The current activity direction is:

1. Find a Rule
2. Crop It Wrong
3. Make the Caption Lie
4. Fool Someone
5. Background Business
6. Use the Edge
7. Before / After
8. Wrong Order
9. Make Me Do Something
10. Say No
11. Use Something Nobody Reads
12. Kill Your Favourite Trick

These activities should collectively build a **Misbehaviour Toolbox** that students can revisit for later assessment work.

## Assessment

Assessment weights are fixed at:

* **Weekly Misbehaviour - 20%**
* **A1: One Rule, Broken - 20%**
* **A2: Read Me Again - 30%**
* **A3: The Badly Behaved Book - 30%**

Total: **100%**

Do not change these weights unless explicitly instructed.

### Weekly Misbehaviour - 20%

Students complete weekly low-risk experiments.

Completing **10 of 12** prompts is enough for the full component.

The component should reward ongoing creative practice, not attendance.

Use a simple completion model rather than a detailed quality rubric.

### A1 - One Rule, Broken - 20%

Students select one picture-book convention and deliberately break it.

The prototype should be approximately 4-6 pages or spread-equivalents.

The key constraint is:

**One rule only.**

Students also provide a short explanation of roughly 150-250 words covering:

* the rule;
* how it was broken;
* the intended reader effect.

Main learning outcomes: LO1, LO2, LO3.

### A2 - Read Me Again - 30%

Students create a short picture-book prototype whose reading changes when experienced again.

This is not limited to plot twists.

It may use:

* background narrative;
* visual clues;
* reading order;
* unreliable narration;
* page relationships;
* reader action;
* perspective;
* withholding or reframing information.

The prototype should be approximately 6-10 pages or spread-equivalents.

Students must test it with approximately 2-3 readers without explaining the trick first.

They should record lightweight evidence of what readers noticed, missed, misunderstood, or reconsidered.

They then provide a short design response of roughly 300 words explaining expected versus observed reader response and resulting revisions.

Main learning outcomes: LO2, LO3, LO4.

### A3 - The Badly Behaved Book - 30%

Students create an original resolved experimental picture-book work.

It should deliberately break one or more conventions to create a coherent reading experience.

Possible formats include:

* physical book;
* digital work;
* photographed prototype;
* collage;
* illustrated prototype;
* interactive or unusual book structure.

Resolution does not mean polish.

The final should normally combine at least two forms of misbehaviour, but only where they support each other.

Do not reward adding techniques simply to create a longer checklist.

The project must include at least one cycle of:

**prototype -> reader test -> revision**

Students also provide a Reader Note of roughly 400 words maximum explaining:

* intended reader experience;
* what happened during testing;
* what changed;
* why the final work breaks the selected rules.

Main learning outcomes: LO3, LO4, LO5.

## Website behaviour

The website may reflect the idea of a misbehaving picture book, but usability comes first.

Use this rule:

**The content may misbehave. The website must not.**

Experimental behaviour must be intentional, legible, and reversible.

Do not sacrifice:

* navigation;
* readability;
* accessibility;
* keyboard access;
* responsive behaviour;
* basic browser reliability;
* understanding of course information;
* assessment clarity.

Any visual or interactive misbehaviour should support the course idea rather than exist as decoration.

Prefer controlled moments of surprise over a constantly chaotic interface.

---

## Visual direction

Avoid a generic colourful children's-book aesthetic.

Prefer a restrained editorial and picture-book visual language with:

* strong typography;
* generous whitespace;
* paper-like or page-like structure where useful;
* simple graphic or illustrative elements;
* deliberate framing and composition;
* controlled irregularity.

The site should feel experimental but still credible as a university course website.

Do not make every section visually noisy.

Do not add decorative complexity that makes the content harder to scan.

## Course-specific interaction ideas

Interactive details may be used selectively where they connect directly to course content.

Examples include:

* framing or cropping effects in Week 2;
* text/image contradiction in Week 3;
* page-edge or gutter behaviour in Week 6;
* page-turn expectation in Week 7;
* ordering or rearrangement in Week 8;
* reader action in Week 9;
* character refusal in Week 10;
* metafiction or book-awareness in Week 11.

These are possibilities, not a checklist.

Do not force an interaction into every week.

A simple page is preferable when an interaction does not improve the concept.

---

## Recurring narrative detail

A small recurring background character or visual narrative may appear across selected weeks.

If used, it should behave like a discoverable secondary story:

* initially appears decorative;
* gradually becomes more active;
* may notice titles, page edges, navigation, or the reader;
* may become self-aware around Weeks 10-11.

This must remain an optional layer.

It must never interfere with navigation, reading, assessment information, or accessibility.

## Content quality

Use clear, concise language.

Avoid:

* generic AI-style motivational language;
* excessive jargon;
* repeated summaries;
* inflated academic phrasing;
* filler paragraphs;
* saying the same idea in several ways;
* vague claims such as "students will explore creativity" without explaining what they actually do.

Course descriptions should explain concrete activities, decisions, reader effects, and design problems.

Examples should support the teaching idea rather than substitute for it.

Do not invent external references, books, readings, links, quotes, or factual claims without verifying them.

Do not invent fictional staff, lecturers, tutors, biographies, credentials, or people. This course does not use a People section unless the user later provides real people who need to appear.

## Process and evidence

This assignment is assessed heavily on process.

Do not make large unexplained batches of changes.

Work in small logical stages.

For each substantial stage:

1. inspect the relevant existing files;
2. make only the changes needed for that stage;
3. run the strongest available local checks;
4. inspect the resulting diff;
5. fix problems before committing;
6. commit with a specific message describing the logical change;
7. push the commit.

Do not combine unrelated changes into one commit.

Do not rewrite or squash existing history unless explicitly asked.

Preserve useful evidence for `PROCESS.md`, including important decisions, revisions, rejected approaches, tests, and meaningful commits.
