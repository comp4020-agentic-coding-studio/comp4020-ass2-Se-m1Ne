Last week controlled one page turn inside a sequence the reader was still
expected to move through in order. Week 8 asks a bigger question: does that
sequence have to stay fixed at all?

- **Reading backwards** — reversing a sequence can change what looks like a
  cause, what looks like a consequence, what seems like a beginning, and what
  the reader knows at each moment. Simply reversing the pages doesn't
  automatically produce a second meaningful story — the reversal has to
  actually change something.
- **Circular reading** — a sequence can return the reader to where it
  started. The ending can change how the beginning reads, or the work can
  deliberately avoid having one definitive starting point. A circle needs a
  reason; repeating something endlessly isn't automatically meaningful.
- **Two-direction reading** — a work can support more than one route through
  it, where each direction creates a different relationship between the same
  events rather than just the same story read forwards or backwards.
- **Reordering** — separate units can be rearranged to change causality,
  emphasis, character interpretation, anticipation, or the ending. The
  question to keep asking is: what actually changes because the order
  changed?
- **Navigation cues** — if a reader can move through the work differently,
  the work still has to show them that. Where can reading begin? What comes
  next? Are multiple routes allowed? Experimental order shouldn't depend on
  the reader stumbling onto it by accident.

There's an important distinction here: non-linear is not the same as random.
If every possible order produces basically the same experience, the
reordering isn't doing narrative work. If every order is incomprehensible,
the reader has nothing to build meaning from. The useful middle ground is a
design where different orders are each interpretable, but interpretable
differently — where something that was a consequence in one order becomes a
cause, a mystery, or a setup in another.

Today's studio, Same Pieces, Different Story, asks you to take six fixed
cards and arrange them into two different, meaningfully different stories —
then design a simple way for a reader to discover that more than one order is
possible.
