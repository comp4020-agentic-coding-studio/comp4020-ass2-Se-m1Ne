A reader normally trusts a story to give them enough truthful information to
work out what's happening. Week 4 asks what happens once that trust becomes
something the storyteller can use — not to trick the reader for its own
sake, but to control what they believe until they have reason to reconsider
it.

There are five tools for this:

- **Unreliable narration** — the narrator's account isn't automatically the
  truth of the story. A narrator can misunderstand, exaggerate, avoid
  something, or only ever give their own version of events.
- **False assumptions** — a story doesn't have to state something false
  outright. It can hand the reader partial information and let them complete
  it themselves, which is a different kind of misdirection to an outright
  lie.
- **Withholding** — delaying information is a real tool, but hiding
  everything is a weak version of it. What's withheld, and when it becomes
  available, should have a reason.
- **Visual misdirection** — framing, scale, background detail, page
  position, and where attention is drawn can all make a reader notice one
  thing while missing another — the same tools from Weeks 2 and 3, now aimed
  at controlling belief rather than just meaning.
- **Limited point of view** — the reader may only know what one character
  can see, understand, remember, or believe. Changing whose view is
  available changes what looks true.

The most important test for any of this is fairness on rereading. After a
reveal, a reader should be able to go back to the earlier pages and find the
clue was actually there — misread, overlooked, or understood differently the
first time. A reveal that depends on information appearing from nowhere
isn't misdirection, it's just withholding with no payoff.

There's a weak version of this idea and a stronger one. Weak: the reader
couldn't possibly have known, because the information wasn't there. Strong:
the reader saw the information and interpreted it differently. Aim for the
second — confusing a reader is not the same as controlling what they
believe.

Today's studio, Read Me Twice, asks you to build a four-page story that reads
one way the first time and differently the second, with the clue for the
second reading sitting in plain sight from the start.
