A picture book's words and its pictures don't have to say the same thing.
Most of the time we assume they do — the text describes what's happening,
and the picture shows it happening. But that relationship is a choice, not a
rule, and changing it changes what the reader has to do to understand the
page.

There are at least three ways words and pictures can relate to each other:

- **Agree** — the picture supports what the words say. This can give a page
  clarity, reinforcement, and certainty, though pure agreement can also leave
  little for the reader to work out between the two.
- **Contradict** — the picture shows something incompatible with what the
  words say. The gap the reader has to fill can affect trust, tension,
  humour, or how well the reader thinks they understand a character — not
  only comedy.
- **Reveal** — the words and picture don't contradict each other, but one
  supplies information the other leaves out. The text might report what a
  character says, while the picture shows something that character hasn't
  noticed.

Difference on its own isn't automatically meaningful. Before making words and
pictures disagree, it's worth asking:

- what does the reader learn from the gap?
- who knows more — the narrator, the character, the image, or the reader?
- does the relationship change how the reader interprets the scene?
- would the page lose something if the words and picture simply matched?

Today's studio holds one sentence fixed and makes three pictures for it — one
that agrees, one that contradicts, and one that reveals something the
sentence never says — so you can compare what each relationship asks of the
reader.
