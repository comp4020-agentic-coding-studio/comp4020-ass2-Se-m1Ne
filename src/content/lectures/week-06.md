A page is normally treated as a neutral surface that just holds the story —
the edge is where the picture stops, the gutter is an awkward fold to work
around, the margin is empty space, and the text sits on top of whatever is
underneath it. Week 6 asks what happens if you stop treating any of that as
neutral.

- **Edge** — the edge marks where visible content stops. Something can
  approach it, be cut off by it, or seem to continue beyond it. Leaving the
  page can be an event, not just a crop.
- **Gutter** — the fold between facing pages is usually an obstacle to design
  around. It can instead become a divide, a gap, a hiding place, or something
  a character has to cross. Not every book uses a bound gutter the same way —
  treat it as a spread-based device, not a fixed rule.
- **Margin** — margins are usually unused or protective space. Something can
  instead enter the margin, hide there, get trapped there, or leave the area
  a reader expects content to stay inside.
- **Text block** — text is normally read as information placed beside or over
  the picture. It can instead have spatial presence of its own: something can
  avoid it, collide with it, hide behind it, or be divided by it.

None of this is about graphic-design software or finding one clever layout
trick. It's about treating the physical structure of the page as material the
story can use, the same way Weeks 2 and 3 treated framing and wording as
material.

Breaking any of these boundaries should still do something. Before you break
one, ask what it means while it holds: what does the edge, gutter, margin, or
text block normally keep in or keep out? Then ask what changes once something
crosses it — what can now be seen, where something can now go, or what a
reader now understands that they didn't before. If the story would work
exactly the same with everything sitting safely inside the normal content
area, the boundary hasn't actually done anything yet.

Today's studio, Escape the Frame, asks you to pick one of these boundaries,
give it a clear role, and build a short sequence where something establishes
that role and then deliberately breaks it.
