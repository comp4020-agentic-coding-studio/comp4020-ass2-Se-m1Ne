A page usually tells you where to look first — through size, position,
contrast, repetition, text, or which character is drawn most prominently.
Week 5 asks what else can be happening on that same page while your
attention is somewhere else.

- **Foreground story** — the apparent primary narrative, established by
  whatever the page makes most obvious. It doesn't need to be literally
  closest to the viewer, just what draws attention first.
- **Background story** — a secondary sequence built from less prominent
  details. To count as a story rather than decoration, it needs to change
  over time, reward attention, and connect across pages.
- **Accumulation** — a background story is often weak on any single page and
  only becomes visible through repetition and change. The useful question
  across a sequence is: what's different from the previous page?
- **Attention** — different readers can follow different routes through the
  same page, picking up different information first. There's no single
  correct viewing order — the framing work from Week 2 still applies here,
  since scale, placement, and space all shape what gets noticed.
- **Rereading** — once a reader notices a background story, a second read of
  the same pages can look different, because they now know where to look.

Two people looking at the same page won't necessarily notice the same
information. One reader might follow the main written story while another
tracks a repeated visual detail, and each can notice something the other
missed. This isn't about age or ability — it's just what it means to design
a page dense enough to support more than one route through it.

A background detail is not automatically a background story. One object
appearing once is still decoration. A real background narrative needs the
reader to be able to track change, consequence, or progression across the
sequence — and not every background event needs to grow into a major plot
either. The goal is controlled secondary meaning, not a second story crammed
into every corner.

Today's studio, Two Readers, Two Stories, asks you to plan a foreground story
and a background story separately, then combine them across a short
sequence, so the background stays missable but rewarding rather than either
invisible or dominant.
