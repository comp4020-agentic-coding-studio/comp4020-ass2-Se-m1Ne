Week 9 gave the reader an action that the story genuinely needed. Week 10
turns the question around: the reader — or the narrator, or the text, or the
shape of the story itself — can expect something from a character, and the
character doesn't have to cooperate.

A useful question to hold onto all week: **who appears to be in control of
the page?**

Expectation can come from several directions.

- **Narrator → character** — a narrator can state or imply what a character
  is about to do. The character can visually or textually resist that
  direction, and the interesting part is the gap between what the narration
  expects and what the character actually does. This is close to Week 3's
  word-image disagreement, applied to a character's behaviour rather than a
  single fact.
- **Text → character** — text on the page can act like an instruction, a
  prediction, a label, a rule, or a boundary. A character can refuse to fit
  what the text says, which turns the text itself into part of the conflict
  rather than a neutral caption.
- **Reader → character** — a work can encourage the reader to expect, invite,
  or attempt something from a character. The character can resist that too.
  This isn't a new interaction mechanism to design, the way Week 9 was — the
  focus stays on how the character responds, not on building a new reader
  action.
- **Story → character** — a familiar story pattern can seem to demand a
  particular next event. A character can refuse to play that expected role.
  This works with a generic, unnamed pattern; it doesn't need a specific
  borrowed story to make the point.

None of this is really about a character saying the word "no." A meaningful
refusal changes something — direction, pacing, who seems to be in charge,
what the reader expected next, how the page is arranged, or what can happen
afterward. The test is simple: **what becomes different because the
character refuses?** If nothing changes, the refusal is just a line of
dialogue.

Put together, the model for the week is:

**Expectation → Refusal → Consequence**

A weak version has the character do something unrelated purely to seem
unpredictable — that's randomness, not resistance. A stronger version
establishes a real expectation, has the character resist it specifically, and
lets that resistance produce a consequence.

Today's studio, Say No to the Story, gives everyone the same starting line —
*"And then the character opened the door."* — and asks what happens when the
character refuses to open it.
