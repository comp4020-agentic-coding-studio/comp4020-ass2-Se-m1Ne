Week 11 asked whether the book could notice the system that contains it.
Week 12 asks a different question: now that you have a toolbox of ways to
misbehave, which of them does your work actually need? The move is **what
else can I break? → what should I keep?** — the last step in the course's
Notice, Break, Construct, Test, Integrate progression.

**More tricks are not automatically better.** A work can contain several
clever ideas and still feel incoherent if they compete for the reader's
attention rather than serving one experience. For any technique in your
work, ask: what is the central experience, does this break support it or
distract from it, and does it earn its place or only prove you know another
trick? Complexity is not the same thing as quality.

**Coherence** means the choices in a work feel like they belong together,
not that they look the same. A coherent work can still contradict itself,
break sequence, involve the reader, resist the narrator, or combine several
techniques — coherence is about whether those choices support one another,
not about reducing a work to a single device.

**Reader effect** returns to one of the course's running questions: what
does this make the reader do, notice, believe, feel, or reconsider? If you
cannot name what a technique changes for the reader, it may not be
necessary.

**Revision**, at this stage, rarely means polishing the same idea. It can
mean removing a device, simplifying it, moving it, combining two
techniques into one, making a cue clearer, reducing an effect, or changing
a sequence. A revision that makes a work look less experimental can still
make it work better.

**Restraint** is choosing not to use an effect the work could support,
because the work is stronger without it. Restraint is not a retreat from
misbehaviour — it is deciding which misbehaviour earns its place.

The test behind all of this is practical: **remove one trick. What
changes?** Take a technique out of a current piece of work and compare the
two versions. If the story or the reader effect gets weaker, or something
important becomes impossible, that is evidence the technique belongs. If
the work becomes clearer without it, that is evidence it does not —
removing it permanently is a legitimate outcome, not a failure.

Across the course you have built ways to notice implicit rules, disrupt
framing, set words and images against each other, mislead a reader on
purpose, run a second story in the background, use page space and the page
turn, rearrange reading order, ask the reader to act, let a character
refuse, and let the book notice its own form. None of that is a checklist
Assignment 3 expects you to complete — it is a set of choices to make
deliberately, using exactly the judgement this week practises.

Today's studio, Remove One Trick, asks you to test one of your own
techniques by taking it out and seeing what your work loses, and what it
gains.
