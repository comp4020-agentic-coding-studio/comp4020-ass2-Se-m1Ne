## Before the session

Bring simple materials for a two-page pair — paper, pens, scissors and old
magazines, or your phone. You're designing one turn, not a long sequence.

## Before / After

This is the main studio activity. Design one before/after page-turn pair —
two page-like units, read in sequence — built around a single primary
effect:

- hide;
- delay;
- reveal;
- surprise;
- a shift in time.

Choose **one** of these rather than trying to demonstrate all of them at
once.

Suggested process:

1. Decide what the reader should believe or expect before the turn.
2. Design the Before page.
3. Identify exactly what information is being withheld by the physical turn.
4. Design the After page.
5. Compare what the reader knows before the turn and after it.
6. Revise if the same effect would work equally well with both pages visible
   at the same time.

Step 6 is the real test. If your idea doesn't depend on information being
hidden until the turn, the page turn probably isn't doing enough work yet.

None of this needs polished illustration. Rough drawing, collage,
photography, typography, or simple shapes are all legitimate.

## Studio check

Once you have your pair, answer briefly:

1. What does the reader know before turning?
2. What do they expect?
3. What is hidden?
4. What becomes visible after the turn?
5. What changes?
6. Why does this idea need a page turn?

Keep the answers short — this is a quick check, not an essay.

## This Week's Misbehaviour — Before / After

This shares its title with the main studio, but it is a different, separate
15–30 minute experiment — not a second constructed pair.

Take one simple event and make two extremely rough versions of where its
page turn happens:

- **Version A** — the turn happens too early.
- **Version B** — the turn happens too late.

Compare how moving the break changes timing, anticipation, clarity, or
surprise. Then answer briefly: which timing works better, and why?

This is about testing where a turn falls, not building another polished
before/after pair — keep both versions quick and rough.

## Afterwards

Add your before/after pair and your turn-timing notes to your Misbehaviour
Toolbox. Week 6 turned the page itself into a place with edges and
boundaries. Week 7 turned the act of leaving one page for the next into
something designed — controlling what the reader knows and when they know
it. Next week asks whether the reader has to move through the pages in one
correct order at all.
