## Before the session

Bring simple materials for a four-page (or four-panel, four-spread) story —
paper, pens, scissors and old magazines, or your phone. This is a small,
quick story, not a finished book.

## Read Me Twice

This is the main studio activity. Build a four-page, or four page-like unit,
micro-story that:

- sets up one initial assumption on the first read — Interpretation A;
- includes at least one clue, visible before the reveal, that a careful
  reader could notice;
- delivers a reveal or reversal that changes what an earlier page means;
- still makes sense on a second read under the new interpretation —
  Interpretation B.

This isn't asking for a dramatic plot twist, polished illustration, or
complicated writing. Four rough pages that control what the reader assumes,
then correct it, is enough.

## Test it

Read your four pages yourself, or better, hand them to someone else, and
check:

**First reading**

- What did you think was happening?
- What made you think that?

**After the reveal**

- What changed?
- Which earlier detail now means something different?

**Rereading**

- Was the clue actually there, or did it only feel that way in hindsight?
- Does Interpretation B actually fit the earlier pages, or does it need new
  information you never showed?

If the reveal only works because something completely new shows up at the
end, go back and plant it earlier instead.

## This Week's Misbehaviour — Fool Someone

A separate 15–30 minute experiment from Read Me Twice, not a second
micro-story:

- make one small piece — a single spread, an image-text pairing, or a very
  short sequence — designed to make someone form one specific wrong
  assumption;
- show it to another person without explaining the trick;
- record what you expected them to assume, what they actually assumed, and
  which detail caused it.

This isn't about embarrassing anyone. It's a quick, low-risk test of how much
control you actually have over what a reader assumes.

## Afterwards

Add your four pages and your Fool Someone notes to your Misbehaviour
Toolbox. Week 3 showed that words and pictures can tell a reader different
things. Week 4 used that same gap, plus withheld information and point of
view, to control what the reader believes until a reveal changes it. Next
week looks at what happens when a second story is running quietly in the
background of the one the reader thinks they're reading.
