## Before the session

Bring one source image you're allowed to reframe — a photograph, a piece of
found imagery you have the rights to use, a simple digital shape, or a rough
drawing of your own. You don't need four images; you need four different ways
of showing one.

## Same Picture, Different Truth

This is the main studio activity. Using your one source image:

- create four different framings of it, changing crop, scale, perspective,
  orientation, or negative space;
- make each version point the reader toward a different meaning, situation,
  emphasis, or assumption than the others;
- keep the underlying source the same throughout — only the framing changes.

None of the four needs to be a polished composition. A rough crop on a
printed photo, four phone screenshots at different zooms, or four paper masks
over one image are all legitimate answers.

Once you have your four, compare them:

- what changed between versions;
- what stayed exactly the same;
- what assumption each framing seems to invite the reader to make.

## This Week's Misbehaviour — Crop It Wrong

A smaller, separate exercise from Same Picture, Different Truth, done in
15–30 minutes:

- make one crop of an image that feels wrong on first look — awkward,
  incomplete, or badly framed;
- test whether that "wrong" crop still does something useful: does it hide
  information, remove the expected subject, leave too much empty space, cut
  something at an uncomfortable point, or push attention onto something
  normally ignored?
- write one or two sentences on why the crop feels wrong, and what it makes a
  reader notice or assume instead.

This is not a fifth framing for Same Picture, Different Truth. It is a
quicker, rougher test of the same idea from the opposite direction — starting
from "this looks like a mistake" instead of "this looks intentional."

## Afterwards

Add your four framings and your Crop It Wrong note to your Misbehaviour
Toolbox. Week 1 asked you to find the rules a picture book usually keeps
invisible. This week you broke one of the most basic ones — that a picture
simply shows what is there — by changing how the same source is framed. Next
week keeps the picture fixed and starts changing what the words say about it
instead.
