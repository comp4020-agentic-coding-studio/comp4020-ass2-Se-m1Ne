## Before the session

Bring simple materials for a short three- to five-unit prototype — paper,
pens, scissors, cards, or your phone. You're making one book feature
necessary to the story, not building a fully bound object.

## The Book Notices Itself

This is the main studio activity. Choose **one** normally ignored book
feature:

- cover;
- spine;
- endpaper;
- page number;
- gutter;
- or another feature, if you can clearly justify it.

Build a short prototype — three to five states, pages, spreads, or
components — where that feature becomes indispensable to the story rather
than decorative. Work through:

1. **Normal role** — what would this feature normally do?
2. **Recognition** — how does the story, character, narrator, or reader
   become aware of it?
3. **Use** — how does the story actually use the feature?
4. **Consequence** — what changes because the feature exists?

Stay with your one chosen feature rather than combining several — a single
feature that the story genuinely needs is worth more than several features
that are each merely noticed.

You don't need a professionally bound physical book. Folded paper, loose
pages, cards, rough mock-ups, simple digital screens, collage, typography,
drawing, or photography are all legitimate, as long as the prototype
communicates enough of the physical relationship to test the idea.

## Studio check

Once you have your prototype, briefly answer:

1. Which normally ignored feature did you choose?
2. What is its normal role?
3. How does the work acknowledge it?
4. What happens because of it?
5. What breaks if you remove it?

Question 5 matters most. If removing the feature leaves the idea working
exactly the same, the feature is probably still decoration — revise until
it is genuinely necessary.

## This Week's Misbehaviour — Use Something Nobody Reads

A separate 15–30 minute experiment from The Book Notices Itself — not
another complete prototype.

Choose one small piece of book infrastructure that readers usually treat as
purely functional — a page number, a contents-like label, a title, a
margin, a running header, an end mark, or another small structural element.
Make one quick page or spread where that element unexpectedly changes how
the page is understood.

Briefly answer:

1. What would the reader normally ignore?
2. What happens when they notice it?

Keep it rough and small — this doesn't need a full metafictional narrative.

## Afterwards

Add your prototype and your Use Something Nobody Reads note to your
Misbehaviour Toolbox. Week 10 had a character resist what the narrator,
text, reader, or story expected. Week 11 asked the book to acknowledge the
system that contains all of them. This is the last week that introduces a
new technique — next week is about deciding which of the tricks you've
collected are actually worth keeping.
