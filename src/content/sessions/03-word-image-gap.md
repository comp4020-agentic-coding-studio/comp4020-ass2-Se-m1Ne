## Before the session

Bring simple materials you can make three quick images with — paper, a pen,
scissors and old magazines, or your phone. Nothing here needs polish.

## The fixed sentence

Use this exact sentence for all three versions below, unchanged:

> She said everything was fine.

## Same Words, Three Pictures

This is the main studio activity. Using the sentence above, make three
separate images:

- **Version A — Agree**: the image supports what the sentence says.
- **Version B — Contradict**: the image makes the sentence look false,
  incomplete, ironic, or unreliable.
- **Version C — Reveal**: the image shows something the sentence never
  mentions.

The sentence stays exactly the same in all three versions — you are not
allowed to solve this by rewriting it.

None of the three need to be polished. Rough drawing, collage, photography,
simple digital shapes, or typography are all legitimate.

Once you have your three versions, compare them:

- what the words contribute on their own;
- what the image contributes on their own;
- what meaning only exists because the two are read together;
- which version asks the reader to do the most interpretive work.

## This Week's Misbehaviour — Make the Caption Lie

A smaller, separate exercise from Same Words, Three Pictures, done in
15–30 minutes:

- start with an image that looks reasonably straightforward;
- write a short caption that makes the reader question, reinterpret, or
  distrust what they think they're seeing — not simply the opposite of what
  the picture shows;
- note briefly what the image seemed to mean before the caption, and what
  changes once it's added.

This is not a fourth version of Same Words, Three Pictures. It works in the
other direction — instead of a fixed sentence guiding three pictures, one
picture gets destabilised by a caption written after the fact.

## Afterwards

Add your three versions and your caption note to your Misbehaviour Toolbox.
Week 2 showed that changing how an image is framed changes what it appears to
tell the reader. Week 3 held the words fixed instead and changed what the
image did with them — agreeing, contradicting, or revealing something the
sentence never said. Next week asks what happens when the story itself can't
be trusted.
