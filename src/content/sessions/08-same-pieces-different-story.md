## Before the session

Bring materials for six small cards or page-like units — paper, pens,
scissors and old magazines, or your phone. You're building one fixed set of
six pieces, then reordering it, not drawing twelve separate images.

## Same Pieces, Different Story

This is the main studio activity. Make six cards, then:

**Story A** — arrange all six cards in one order that produces one readable
story or interpretation.

**Story B** — rearrange the same six cards into a different order that
produces a meaningfully different story or interpretation.

Requirements:

- all six cards are used in both versions;
- the content of the cards does not change — only the order does;
- Story B has to be more than Story A told awkwardly; the new order should
  change causality, emphasis, character interpretation, the ending, or
  another clear narrative relationship.

Record both orders, for example `A → C → F → B → D → E` and
`D → B → A → E → C → F` — these are just examples of how to notate your own
two orders, not sequences to copy.

None of this needs polished illustration. Rough drawing, collage,
photography, typography, or simple shapes are all legitimate.

## Let the reader choose

Once you have Story A and Story B, design a simple way for a reader to
encounter the six cards in different orders themselves — loose cards, a
branching choice, two marked starting points, a reversible direction, or
plain instructions for rearranging them. This doesn't need to be
technically complex; the important question is how the reader knows that
another order is possible at all.

## Test it

Give your six-card system to another reader without explaining Story A or
Story B first. Notice:

1. Where do they start?
2. What order do they choose or construct?
3. What story do they think happened?
4. Do they realise another order is possible?
5. What changes when they try a second order?

Keep this light — it's a quick observation, not the full reader testing later
assessments will ask for.

## This Week's Misbehaviour — Wrong Order

A separate 15–30 minute experiment from Same Pieces, Different Story — not
another six-card project.

Take a tiny familiar sequence of three moments, `1 → 2 → 3`, and present it in
a wrong order instead, such as `3 → 1 → 2`. Then ask:

- what does the new first moment make the reader assume?
- does something that was originally a consequence now feel like a cause, a
  mystery, or a setup?
- what new meaning appears?

Briefly note the expected order, the wrong order you used, and what changed
between them. Keep it rough and quick.

## Afterwards

Add your six-card set and your Wrong Order note to your Misbehaviour Toolbox.
Week 7 controlled what happened after one page turn inside a sequence the
reader still moved through in order. Week 8 questioned whether that sequence
had to stay fixed at all. Next week goes further again: the reader's own
action becomes something the story actually needs to happen.
