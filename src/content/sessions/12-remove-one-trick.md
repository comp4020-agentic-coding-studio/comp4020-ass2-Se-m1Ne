## Before the session

Bring a current experimental picture-book concept, prototype, sequence, or
substantial fragment — work developing toward the final assignment, or
another prototype from this course. You are revising something that
already exists, not starting a new story.

## Remove One Trick

This is the main studio activity, in three stages.

### 1. Identify

Briefly name:

1. the main rule your work breaks;
2. what that break is supposed to do for the reader;
3. any other tricks or experimental devices currently present.

Keep this short — a few sentences per point is enough.

### 2. Remove

Choose **one** noticeable trick or device and make a temporary version of
the work without it. Actually remove, replace, hide, or neutralise it —
don't just imagine what the change would be. You need two versions you can
genuinely compare, not one version and a description of the other.

### 3. Compare

Set Version A (with the trick) beside Version B (without it) and ask:

- Which version communicates the central idea more clearly?
- What does the reader lose?
- What does the reader gain?
- Does the trick support the work, or compete with it?

The honest answer is allowed to be **remove it permanently**. Not every
experimental device is required to survive revision.

## Peer review

Show a peer the work without a long explanation first. Ask them three
questions, in this order:

1. What rule do you think this work is breaking?
2. What exactly is the break?
3. What effect does it have on you as a reader?

That's **rule → break → effect**. Then compare their reading with your own
intention. If they notice a different trick first, that's useful evidence
about what's actually competing for attention in the work — not a reason
to explain what they were supposed to see.

## Revision decision

Using the removal test and the peer review together, make one decision
about the trick you tested: **keep**, **remove**, **reduce**, **clarify**,
or **replace**. State briefly why. The point isn't to defend your original
design — it's to make the work stronger.

## This Week's Misbehaviour — Kill Your Favourite Trick

A separate 15–30 minute experiment from Remove One Trick — not another
version of the same comparison.

Pick a technique from any previous week that you personally enjoy using.
For one small piece or fragment, ban yourself from using it, then solve the
same narrative problem another way. For example: if you normally rely on
contradiction, solve it without contradiction; if you normally use a
reveal turn, solve it without one; if you normally use reader interaction,
solve it without asking the reader to act. These are examples, not a menu
— pick whichever habit is actually yours.

Briefly answer:

1. What favourite trick did you remove?
2. What did you use instead?
3. What did the restriction reveal about your habits?

This is about noticing a habit, not producing a polished piece.

## Afterwards

Add your comparison and your Kill Your Favourite Trick note to your
Misbehaviour Toolbox — the last thing that goes in it. The toolbox was
never a checklist to complete; Assignment 3 asks you to choose from it
deliberately, not exhaustively. The goal was never to break every rule. It
was to know which rule to break, why, and what happens to the reader when
you do.
