## Before the session

Bring simple materials for a short three- to five-unit sequence — paper,
pens, scissors, cards, or your phone. You're building one refusal and its
consequence, not a fully resolved story.

## Say No to the Story

This is the main studio activity. Every sequence starts with exactly this
line, unchanged at its first use:

> "And then the character opened the door."

The character refuses to open the door. Build a short sequence — three to
five page/spread-like units — that shows:

1. the expectation that the door will be opened;
2. the character's refusal;
3. the story, narrator, or text responding to that refusal;
4. a consequence;
5. a changed direction or relationship by the end.

Not every step needs its own page — some can share a unit. The line itself
should stay recognisable; the work around it is what has to behave
differently.

The character doesn't have to say the word "no." Refusal can come through
action, image, placement, scale, the page boundary, a disagreement between
text and image, interruption, avoidance, or another clear decision — the
refusal just has to be understandable from the work itself. Earlier
techniques are available here if they help: Week 3's word-image gap, Week
6's page boundaries, Week 9's reader action. None of them is required, and
one clear refusal is worth more than several stacked together.

None of this needs polished drawing. Rough drawing, collage, cut paper,
typography, or simple shapes are all legitimate.

## Studio check

Once you have your sequence, briefly answer:

1. What was the character expected to do?
2. Who or what created that expectation?
3. How did the character refuse?
4. What changed because of the refusal?
5. Who seems to have more control by the end?

If your answer to question 4 is close to "nothing," the refusal is probably
still just dialogue — revise until the refusal actually changes something.

## This Week's Misbehaviour — Say No

A separate 15–30 minute experiment from Say No to the Story — not another
door sequence.

Pick one simple instruction that could plausibly appear in a story —
structurally something like "Sit here," "Go this way," "Wait," or "Look
up." These are just examples; choose your own. Make one quick image or page
where a character clearly refuses that instruction **without using the word
"no."**

Briefly note:

1. what the instruction was;
2. how a reader can tell the character refused;
3. what changed because of the refusal.

Keep it rough and quick.

## Afterwards

Add your sequence and your Say No note to your Misbehaviour Toolbox. Week 9
made the reader's action something the story needed. Week 10 asked whether
the character has to cooperate with what the reader, the narrator, or the
story expects at all. Next week pushes further still: the book starts to
notice it is a book.
